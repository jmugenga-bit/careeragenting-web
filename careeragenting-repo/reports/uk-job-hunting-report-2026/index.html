The State of UK Job Hunting 2026

A clear, sourced picture of what job hunting in the UK actually looks like right now — for young people, graduates, and anyone trying to get back to work — and why good, free help matters more than ever. Every figure below is from a named public source and dated.

By the Career Agenting System team · Culture & Clarity Limited · Published August 2026

Job hunting in 2026 is harder than the headline unemployment rate suggests. Vacancies are falling, more than a million young people are out of work and learning, graduate hiring has cooled, and roughly one in five working-age people are outside the labour market altogether. At the same time, the help that makes the biggest difference — someone in your corner who knows how to build a CV, decode an advert and prepare for an interview — is expensive to buy and stretched thin where it's free.

We pulled together the most recent public data to show the picture plainly. This is a synthesis of official statistics, not a survey of our own; you can follow every number back to its source at the foot of the page.

1. The labour market right now

The UK unemployment rate was 4.9% in March to May 2026, with the employment rate at 75.1% and the economic inactivity rate at 20.9% — meaning around one in five people aged 16 to 64 were neither working nor looking for work. Job vacancies fell again, to an estimated 712,000 in April to June 2026, down 7,000 on the previous quarter — fewer openings for more applicants to compete over.1

2. Young people are bearing the brunt

An estimated 1,012,000 young people aged 16 to 24 were not in education, employment or training (NEET) in January to March 2026 — 13.5% of all young people, and up 89,000 on the year before. Of those, around 400,000 were unemployed and looking for work, while 613,000 were economically inactive. The rate was higher for young men (14.4%) than young women (12.5%).2

Behind each number is a person at the very start of their working life, often without a CV, a network, or anyone to practise an interview with.

3. Graduate hiring has cooled

New graduates felt the slowdown too. Among those who finished in 2023/24 and were surveyed about 15 months later, 81% were in some form of work — but full-time employment slipped to 57%, down two percentage points on the year before, and 7% were unemployed (up from 6%). Of those in work, 75% were in high-skilled roles, a shade below the previous year. Analysts called it 'a cooling, not a collapse' — a subdued economy rather than a shift away from hiring graduates.34

4. Getting back to work is a national priority

With economic inactivity at 20.9% of working-age people, helping those who can and want to work move back into it is a stated national priority. Long-term sickness has become the most commonly reported reason people give for being outside the labour market, and it remains at historically high levels.5 For many people returning after illness, caring, redundancy or a long gap, the hardest part isn't willingness — it's rebuilding a CV, explaining a gap with confidence, and getting through an interview after time away.

5. The careers-support gap

Here's the catch. The help that most improves someone's chances — personal, one-to-one careers support — is either stretched or costly. The free, government-funded National Careers Service exists, but demand is high and in-person one-to-one time is limited.6 Private career coaching fills the gap for those who can pay: publicly listed UK fees typically start around £75–£100 for a single session, with structured programmes costing far more.7

So the people who most need help — young people not yet in work, and those getting back to it after a hard stretch — are often the least able to buy it. That gap, between who needs support and who can afford it, is the reason we built a free career team in the first place.

What we take from this

The 2026 picture is a tighter market with fewer vacancies, more than a million young people out of work and learning, cooler graduate hiring, and a large group trying to get back in — all set against careers support that's either paid-for or overstretched. None of that is solved by a single app, and we don't claim it is. But it does make the case for good help being free, available any time, and built for the UK. That's what we're trying to do: a CV specialist, an applications guide, an interview coach, a steady companion for the hard days, and help to grow once you're in a role — free to start, with no sign-up.

Methodology

This report is a synthesis of publicly available official statistics, not a survey conducted by Career Agenting System. Labour market and NEET figures are from the Office for National Statistics; graduate figures are from the Higher Education Statistics Agency (HESA) Graduate Outcomes release; the note on long-term sickness is from the House of Commons Library; and the careers-support figures reference the National Careers Service and publicly listed UK coaching fees. Every figure states the period it covers. Statistics are estimates and subject to revision by the original publishers; where a figure is not from the most recent possible release, we say so. If you spot anything that needs correcting, please tell us.

Frequently asked questions

How many young people in the UK are NEET?

An estimated 1,012,000 young people aged 16 to 24 were not in education, employment or training (NEET) in January to March 2026 — 13.5% of all young people — according to the ONS. Around 613,000 of them were economically inactive and 400,000 were unemployed.

What is the UK unemployment rate in 2026?

The ONS estimated the UK unemployment rate at 4.9% for people aged 16 and over in March to May 2026, with the economic inactivity rate at 20.9%.

Are graduates struggling to find work?

Graduate hiring cooled rather than collapsed. Of 2023/24 graduates surveyed about 15 months on, 81% were in some form of work but full-time employment fell to 57% and unemployment rose to 7%, according to HESA.
