Just laid off in California? Here's your playbook.

Whether the email landed at 6 a.m. or you're planning your next move, this is a clear, step-by-step guide for California — what to do first, the rights and money you're entitled to, and how to line up your next job. Free to use, and honest about what's information versus advice.

The first 72 hours after a layoff

The shock is real — do these four things and you'll be on solid ground.

  • Your final paycheck is due right away. In California, if you're laid off or fired, your employer must pay everything you're owed — including any accrued, unused vacation or PTO, which California treats as earned wages that can't be forfeited — immediately, at the time of termination. Paying it late can trigger a penalty of up to 30 days' pay.
  • File for unemployment straight away. California's EDD pays roughly 60–70% of your prior earnings, from a minimum of $40 up to a maximum of $450 a week, for up to 26 weeks. There's a one-week unpaid waiting period, so the sooner you file, the sooner money starts. File online at edd.ca.gov or call 1-800-300-5616.
  • Sort out health cover. Losing your job is a qualifying life event, so you can enroll in a Covered California plan outside open enrollment, or continue your employer plan through COBRA — compare the real costs before deciding.
  • Don't rush to sign anything. Severance almost always asks you to sign a release giving up the right to sue. You're allowed to take time to read it — and to have it reviewed — before you sign.

Your rights when you're let go in California

California gives you more protection than most states. The essentials:

  • At-will, but with real limits. California is an at-will state — either side can usually end employment at any time. But you cannot be fired for an unlawful reason: discrimination, retaliation, or for exercising a legal right. The California Civil Rights Department handles those complaints.
  • Big layoffs come with notice. Under California's WARN Act, larger employers must give 60 days' advance written notice before a mass layoff (50 or more people at one site), a plant closing, or certain relocations — broader than the federal rule. If a big layoff gave you little or no notice, it's worth checking your rights (see the EDD WARN pages below).
  • Severance isn't required by law in California — it's discretionary. If it's offered, weigh what you'd give up (usually the right to sue) against what you'd get.

This is general information, not legal advice. For your own situation, use the official California resources at the end of this page, or speak with an employment attorney.

Money and leverage while you search

Two things Californians often leave on the table:

  • Unemployment is yours to claim — file the day you're let go (details above). If you pick up part-time or gig work, report it; you may still qualify for a reduced benefit.
  • Use California's pay-transparency law. Since 2023, employers with 15 or more employees must include the pay range in every job posting — including on third-party sites like LinkedIn and Indeed. You can also ask for the pay scale of a role you're applying for, or your own current role. Use those real numbers to target the right jobs and negotiate from fact, not a guess.

Your résumé for the California market

California hiring — especially in tech — is ATS-first. To get past the software and in front of a human:

  • Mirror the exact keywords in the posting where they're genuinely true of you.
  • Keep the formatting clean — no tables, columns or graphics that trackers can't read. Save as PDF unless asked otherwise.
  • Lead with achievements and metrics, not duties. One page early-career, two if you're senior. No photo, birth date or age.

Frequently asked questions

How much is unemployment in California, and for how long?

California's EDD pays roughly 60–70% of your prior earnings — a minimum of $40 and a maximum of $450 a week — for up to 26 weeks, after a one-week unpaid waiting period. File at edd.ca.gov or call 1-800-300-5616.

When do I get my final paycheck if I'm laid off in California?

Immediately, at the time of termination — including any accrued, unused vacation or PTO, which California counts as earned wages. If you quit with at least 72 hours' notice, it's due on your last day; without notice, within 72 hours. Late final pay can incur a waiting-time penalty of up to 30 days' wages.

Do California employers have to post the salary?

Yes. Since 2023 (SB 1162), employers with 15 or more employees must include the pay range in job postings, including on third-party sites. You can also ask for the pay scale of a role you're applying for, or your current role.

Is California an at-will state?

Yes — either side can usually end employment at any time. But you can't be fired for an unlawful reason such as discrimination, retaliation, or exercising a legal right; those are handled by the California Civil Rights Department.

Do I get advance notice before a layoff in California?

Larger employers must give 60 days' advance written notice before a mass layoff (50 or more people at one site), a plant closing, or certain relocations, under California's WARN Act — broader than the federal WARN Act.

Is this legal or financial advice?

No. It's general information to help you take the next step, current as of August 2026 and based on the official California sources listed on this page. For your own situation, check those sources or speak with a qualified attorney or advisor.
