How to use it

  1. Fill in your situation now — single or a couple, your age, how many children you claim for, your monthly rent, and whether you get the carer or health element.
  2. Enter the job you're thinking about — the pay and how often it's paid (per hour, week, month or year). If it's hourly, add the hours a week.
  3. Press the button to see your monthly income out of work next to your income in work (take-home pay plus the Universal Credit you'd still receive), and roughly how much better off you'd be.

How Universal Credit works when you start a job

A common worry is that every £1 you earn is taken straight off your benefits. It isn't. On Universal Credit, if you have children or a limited capability for work you get a work allowance — an amount you can earn before your UC is touched at all. After that, your Universal Credit only goes down by 55p for every extra £1 you take home, so you always keep at least 45p in the pound. That's why working almost always leaves you with more money overall.

What the figures are based on

The estimate uses 2026/27 Universal Credit rates published by GOV.UK and confirmed in the House of Commons Library uprating briefing: the standard allowance, child element (the two-child limit was removed in April 2026, so every child counts), carer and health elements, the £710 / £427 work allowances, the 55% taper and the benefit cap. Take-home pay uses the same 2026/27 Income Tax and National Insurance rates as our take-home pay calculator.

It's an estimate for guidance only — not financial or benefits advice. It assumes you're on Universal Credit now and uses your rent as your housing element, so your real figures can differ (for example if your rent is capped by Local Housing Allowance, you're still on legacy benefits, or your health element is a protected rate). It doesn't include Council Tax Reduction or childcare support, which usually make working pay even more. For a personalised check, use the GOV.UK benefits calculators or talk to your Jobcentre work coach or Citizens Advice.

Frequently asked questions

Will I really be better off working?

Usually, yes. Universal Credit is designed so that working pays: you keep a work allowance (if you have children or a health condition) and then only lose 55p of UC for each extra £1 you take home — so you always keep something from every hour you work. This tool gives you an estimate for your own situation, but a Jobcentre work coach or Citizens Advice can confirm it for free.

Is this financial advice?

No. It's general information to help you weigh up a decision, based on published 2026/27 rates. It isn't financial or benefits advice, and your actual entitlement can differ. Before deciding, check with the official GOV.UK benefits calculators, your Jobcentre work coach, or a free adviser such as Citizens Advice.

How accurate is it?

It's a good guide for common situations, but it's an estimate. It assumes you're on Universal Credit, uses your rent as your housing element, and doesn't include Council Tax Reduction or childcare support (both of which usually help you more in work). Complex cases — capped rents, legacy benefits, protected health rates — can differ, so treat the number as a starting point, not a promise.

Does it cover the two-child limit?

Yes. The two-child limit was removed in April 2026, so the calculator includes a child element for every child you claim for, including third and later children.

Does it store any of my information?

No. Everything runs entirely in your browser — nothing you enter is uploaded, saved or shared.
